Kerri’s Story

KERRI’S STORY

My why. Our purpose.

This is not my whole story, but it is the part that shaped my voice and advocacy.

As a minor, I was groomed and abused by an authority figure connected to a Kenosha private school. At the time, I did not fully understand what was happening to me. Like many children and teenagers who experience grooming, I carried confusion, shame, fear, and pain I did not yet have words for. Despite documented concerns, no mandated report was made and my parents were never notified.

Years later, as a counselor and mandated reporter, I chose to report what happened to me because I believed it could help protect other children.

I was wrong.

What I found was a system that was not built for reports like mine.

I went to school and church leadership in confidence, believing they would protect children first. Instead, I was blamed while the institution chose to protect itself.

Because I was an adult at the time of reporting, Wisconsin CPS could not investigate, even though the individual remained in regular contact with minors at the school.

The school board and church leadership changed his status from employee to contractor while he continued working around children. I later learned concerns involving this individual had been raised within the school and connected church community for more than 20 years.

I made a police report. No criminal charges were filed due to jurisdictional limitations, not credibility or lack of harm. That same year, he was publicly praised and featured in the school’s 2021 yearbook while the school and church remained in good standing with accrediting bodies.

That reality changed me.

It led me to testify before the Wisconsin State Senate and contribute to the creation of Wisconsin Act 200.

My faith never wavered. My trust in the systems meant to protect children did.

Wisconsin child protective systems are among the few in the nation that do not accept adult survivor reports or operate under a centralized mandated reporting system. These gaps can allow predators to retain access to minors and relocate between counties or states undetected.

It is also worth mentioning that in 2020, a local news outlet interviewed me and several alumni with similar experiences but chose not to publish the story. When Wisconsin Act 200 later passed, the same outlet publicly named me without identifying the institution involved, leaving the community without the full truth.

Today, I am a wife to my loving husband Zac, a mom to my cat Ellie, a godmother, a foster parent, and a Licensed Professional Counselor in Wisconsin specializing in child abuse and neglect.

Kenosha is a beautiful community. I believe in a future where Wisconsin children are among the most protected in the nation, not the least.

Reporting to the same systems that failed to protect me and many others is not easy, but I continue to show up because change matters.

Churches and schools should be among the safest places for children and held to the highest standards of accountability and child protection, not exempt from them.

Project Worth is built on lived experience and on the belief that children deserve stronger protection, survivors deserve to be heard, and systems can change when people are willing to tell the truth with courage and grace.

If you have been hurt, you are not alone.

This is bigger than my case. It is for every child who deserved protection, every survivor who deserved to be heard, and every community willing to do better.

Project Worth exists because children deserve better, and systems must do better.

For more on my advocacy and SB 333, please visit the Media & Advocacy section or review Wisconsin Act 200.

How Wisconsin’s Systems Failed Me

When I came forward, I expected protection. Instead, I was often treated like I was the problem.

I was denied a support person during parts of the investigative process while being forced to repeatedly relive deeply traumatic experiences alone. I had to carry my own case from agency to agency because Wisconsin’s systems often operate in silos instead of as a coordinated protection response. There was no clear guidance, no advocate walking beside me, and no cohesive system focused on survivor safety.

During testimony on Wisconsin Senate Bill 333, a state senator asked whether I had ever been offered a protective order, meaningful safety guidance, or any protective measures by the detective handling my case. I explained that I had not. The detective also declined to refer the case to the District Attorney’s Office, despite the report involving more than 20 years of documented safety concerns related to minors and individuals who remained in contact with children through the school and connected church.

The way my case was handled often made me feel like my abuse was being minimized and treated more as a procedural burden to the system than as a serious crime against a child. Because I was an Illinois resident as a minor and aspects of the grooming occurred across state lines, my case could have warranted some level of cross jurisdictional review or inquiry, yet I was never even asked about it.

I later learned that accrediting bodies received the police report before I did, which was how I discovered the case had been closed. The report concluded no crime had occurred, despite Illinois and federal law already recognizing grooming and child exploitation without physical contact as crimes at the time my abuse occurred.

At one point, the police report connected to my abuse was accessed during a foster licensing process, despite the fact that reports involving minors are generally treated as highly protected information. Experiences like this reinforced how inconsistent and fragile Wisconsin’s protections can sometimes feel for survivors.

In 2026, I independently reported the abuse to Illinois and federal authorities. Within a matter of weeks, I experienced more trauma informed care, due diligence, communication, and seriousness toward my case than I had received from Wisconsin systems in over eight years. Illinois DCFS centrally logged my report of abuse that primarily occurred in Wisconsin between 2010 and 2012. Kenosha CPS has no record of it.

The process with Wisconsin systems was degrading, humiliating, and left me feeling even more powerless and vulnerable than I already was.

Wisconsin is often viewed as more defense friendly than survivor centered. Too often, abused children are expected to navigate systems alone while the adults and institutions around them receive greater procedural protection than the children harmed.

Project Worth exists because children and survivors deserve better than that.

What is Grooming?

Grooming is an abuse of power and trust. It uses authority, access, secrecy, emotional control, and boundary testing to make a child or young person feel isolated, indebted, confused, or responsible for the relationship.

When the adult is an educator, clergy member, counselor, coach, youth leader, or another trusted authority figure, the harm is intensified by the imbalance of power and by any failure of protective adults or institutions to respond.

Recognize patterns early
Signs of Grooming

One behavior alone may not tell the whole story, but patterns matter. Communities should pause, document, report, and protect when adults use access, authority, trust, or secrecy to create control.

Special attention, gifts, favoritism, or private communication
Secrets, blurred boundaries, or requests not to tell parents or other adults
Testing physical, emotional, or digital boundaries over time
Creating dependence, guilt, loyalty, fear, or confusion
Isolating the child from peers, family, or other trusted adults
Minimizing concerns as harmless, misunderstood, or “just words”

It is never “just words” when authority, access, secrecy, and control are used to blur boundaries with a child.

Project Worth supports stronger protections where adults hold spiritual, educational, professional, coaching, mentoring, or caregiving authority over children and vulnerable people.

Why this matters: Abuse of authority can silence children and survivors. Stronger standards help protect children before harm continues.

4Semicolon and sunflower mental health awareness

Semicolon and sunflower mental health awareness for children, teens, and survivors.

Why this matters: Healing work includes hope, visibility, and support for those carrying trauma.

5A centralized mandated reporting system for Wisconsin

A centralized mandated reporting system for Wisconsin, including clear pathways for adults reporting childhood abuse when the person of concern currently has access to minors.

Why this matters: Centralized intake and documentation can help systems retain reports, identify patterns, and respond sooner.

6Policy work informed by stronger protections in other states

Policy work informed by stronger protections in other states so Wisconsin can become one of the safest states for children.

Why this matters: Wisconsin can learn from models that report sooner, document better, and protect children more consistently.

7Current System Gaps

Project Worth advocates for systems that document concerns, retain reports, share information, and act on patterns of risk.

Current gaps can include no centralized mandated reporting system, limited pathways for adult survivor reports, inconsistent civil pathways, and weak tracking of concerning behavior patterns.

Proposed approach: Centralized intake, retained reports, cross jurisdiction communication, and protocols that prioritize risk patterns, not just charges.

8Strengthening Civil Protections

Project Worth supports strengthening survivor centered civil protections, including higher recoverable damages in child abuse cases.

Why this matters: The harm from child abuse can affect safety, mental health, education, relationships, employment, and long term wellbeing.

9Statute of Limitations Reform

Project Worth supports statute of limitations reform so survivors of childhood abuse are not blocked from justice because disclosure, understanding, or safety came later.

Why this matters: Trauma, fear, delayed disclosure, and institutional pressure can prevent earlier action.

10Survivor-Centered Protocols

Survivor-centered protocols help ensure reports are documented, retained, and acted upon in ways that prioritize safety, dignity, and accountability.

Why this matters: When systems respond consistently, survivors are not left carrying what institutions should have addressed.

11Advocacy Ask

We are asking for attention, review, and action through state-level policy review, legislative attention, institutional accountability standards, and community partnerships.

Why this matters: Prevention is not one program. It is the work of building systems that recognize risk, respond clearly, and protect children sooner.

🛡️Institutional Accountability Standards

Project Worth supports clearer accountability standards for schools, churches, youth programs, and child serving institutions when warning signs, misconduct concerns, or reports are raised.

Why this matters: Institutions trusted with children should be expected to document concerns, respond responsibly, and prioritize child safety over reputation.
