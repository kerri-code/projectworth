Kerri’s Story

KERRI’S STORY

My why. Our purpose.

This is not my whole story, but it is the part that shaped my voice and my commitment to change.

As a minor, I was groomed and abused by an authority figure in a Kenosha private school. Despite documented concerns, no mandated report was made and my parents were not notified.

Years later, as a counselor and mandated reporter, I chose to report what happened to me believing it would help protect other children.

I was wrong.

Because I was an adult at the time of reporting, Wisconsin CPS could not investigate, even though the individual remained in regular contact with minors at the school.

I later learned that concerns involving this individual had been raised within the school and its connected church community for over 20 years. I made a police report. No criminal charges were filed due to jurisdictional limitations, not because of credibility or lack of harm.

The same year, the individual was publicly praised and featured in the school’s 2021 yearbook, while the school and church remained in good standing with accrediting bodies.

That reality changed me.

It led me to testify before the Wisconsin State Senate and contribute to the creation of Wisconsin Act 200.

My faith never wavered. My trust in the systems meant to protect children did.

Wisconsin child protective systems are among few in the nation that do not accept adult survivor reports or have a centralized mandated reporting system. These gaps legally retain a predator's access to minors and allow them to relocate to other counties or states undetected.

It is worth mentioning that in 2020, a local news outlet interviewed me and several alumni with similar experiences but did not publish the story. When Wisconsin Act 200 later passed, the same outlet named me without identifying the institution, leaving the community without the full truth.

Today, I am a wife to my loving husband, a mom to my cat Ellie, a foster parent, a professional counselor, and a friend and daughter. Kenosha is a beautiful community. I believe in a future where Wisconsin children are among the most protected in the nation, not the least.

Reporting to the same systems that failed to protect me and many others is not easy, but I continue to show up because change matters.

If you have been hurt, you are not alone.

This is bigger than my case. It is for every child who deserved protection, every survivor who deserved to be heard, and every community willing to do better.

Project Worth exists because children deserve better, and systems must do better.

For more on my advocacy and SB 333, please visit the Media & Advocacy section or review Wisconsin Act 200.

“Every time I thought about giving up, I thought about who this could help, and I knew it was worth it to keep going.

TL;DR: Abuse was reported. Systems failed to act. The risk continued. That failure became the foundation for advocacy, policy change, and Project Worth, so known patterns lead to protection, not silence.

What is Grooming?

Grooming is an abuse of power and trust. It uses authority, access, secrecy, emotional control, and boundary testing to make a child or young person feel isolated, indebted, confused, or responsible for the relationship.

When the adult is an educator, clergy member, counselor, coach, youth leader, or another trusted authority figure, the harm is intensified by the imbalance of power and by any failure of protective adults or institutions to respond.

Recognize patterns early
Signs of Grooming

One behavior alone may not tell the whole story, but patterns matter. Communities should pause, document, report, and protect when adults use access, authority, trust, or secrecy to create control.

Tap each card to expand.

Special attention, gifts, favoritism, or private communication
Secrets, blurred boundaries, or requests not to tell parents or other adults
Testing physical, emotional, or digital boundaries over time
Creating dependence, guilt, loyalty, fear, or confusion
Isolating the child from peers, family, or other trusted adults
Minimizing concerns as harmless, misunderstood, or “just words”

It is never “just words” when authority, access, secrecy, and control are used to blur boundaries with a child.

★ Did You Know?

Wisconsin is behind many states in mandated reporting and child protection accountability.

Click each issue to see where Wisconsin falls short, what stronger states are already doing, and why Project Worth is pushing for reform that protects children sooner.

1Wisconsin is in the minority without a centralized statewide reporting system ⌄

Wisconsin reports are handled through individual county and tribal agencies, while many states use statewide hotlines, centralized intake, or state registry models.

Why this matters: County based reporting can lead to inconsistent screening, documentation, follow through, and pattern tracking.

Sources: Wisconsin DCF CPS Process · Wisconsin DCF Report Abuse · Child Welfare Information Gateway State Reporting Numbers
2Many states now require all adults or all persons to report suspected child abuse ⌄

Wisconsin limits mandated reporting to listed professionals and child serving roles. Many states require any person, or every adult, to report suspected child abuse, including Indiana, Texas, New Jersey, Delaware, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, Tennessee, and Wyoming.

Why this matters: Children often disclose to adults outside official job titles. Child safety should be a shared responsibility.

Sources: Wisconsin Mandatory Reporting Summary · Indiana · Texas · New Jersey · Delaware · Tennessee
3Wisconsin largely focuses on family child abuse and neglect ⌄

Wisconsin CPS largely centers abuse or neglect involving a parent, caregiver, or person responsible for child welfare concerns.

This can leave less clear accountability for clergy, educators, coaches, youth leaders, volunteers, and other trusted adults outside the home who still have power and access to children.

Why this matters: Children can be harmed by authority figures even when abuse is not happening inside the family system.

Sources: Wisconsin Mandatory Reporting Summary · Child Welfare Information Gateway Mandated Reporting · Clergy Reporting Laws
4Adult survivor reports can still fall through major gaps ⌄

Wisconsin CPS is designed for current child safety concerns. Adult survivor reports may not trigger investigation or clear retention, even when the person of concern still has access to children.

Why this matters: Adult disclosures can reveal missed patterns. Systems that ignore them risk missing repeat offenders.

Sources: Wisconsin DCF CPS Process · Child Welfare Information Gateway
5Some states impose stronger accountability when professionals fail to report ⌄

Wisconsin failure to report penalties are limited compared with states that impose stronger consequences for professionals or adults working with children.

Texas, Pennsylvania, New York, and California provide examples of stronger penalties, civil liability, repeat offense penalties, or enhanced consequences for concealment.

Why this matters: Adults entrusted with children should face meaningful accountability when warning signs are ignored.

Sources: Wisconsin Penalties · Texas · Pennsylvania · New York · California
6Authority based relationship protections remain uneven across states ⌄

Wisconsin Act 200 strengthened protections, but Project Worth supports continued reform recognizing that power imbalances do not automatically end at 18.

Washington criminalizes sexual intercourse by a school employee with an enrolled student who is at least 16 and not more than 21 when the employee is at least 60 months older. Enough Abuse reports that more than 75% of states have passed educator sexual misconduct laws.

Why this matters: Authority, trust, dependence, and access can be used to manipulate consent.

Sources: Wisconsin Act 200 · Washington RCW 9A.44.093 · Enough Abuse Educator Misconduct Map
7Civil Protection Gaps ⌄

Civil accountability can be difficult in Wisconsin when cases involve grooming, abuse of authority, delayed disclosure, or institutional patterns that do not fit neatly within existing precedent.

In this case, Illinois offered a stronger civil pathway than Wisconsin, even though the reported abuse occurred primarily in Wisconsin.

Project Worth supports survivor centered civil protections, including higher recoverable damages for child abuse cases. Financial harms such as fraud, property damage, or business disputes can have clearer recovery pathways than grooming based child abuse claims.

Statutes of limitation can also block justice. Illinois and Minnesota allow civil childhood sexual abuse claims at any time, and Minnesota previously created a Child Victims Act lookback window for expired claims.

Many states now recognize that trauma, fear, delayed disclosure, or institutional pressure can prevent earlier action.

Sources: Illinois 735 ILCS 5/13-202.2 · Minnesota Stat. 541.073 · NCSL Civil SOL Summary · Minnesota Child Victims Act Coverage

Why Response Matters

Reports Deserve Documentation, Pattern Review, and Protective Action

These truths belong together: delayed disclosure is common, false reports are uncommon, and child protection systems should be built to respond before harm continues.

Delayed Disclosure Is Common

Disclosure often occurs years later in adulthood, which is a well-documented pattern among survivors of child sexual abuse.

Systems must be built to recognize risk and act early.

False Reports Are Rare

Research estimates false report rates around 2-8%.

Reports should be documented carefully, reviewed for patterns, and taken seriously.

EVAWI; NSVRC, Lisak et al.

Systems Must Carry the Responsibility

Because delayed disclosure is common and false reports are uncommon, systems should prioritize child safety, documentation, and early intervention.

Systems should not rely on survivors to expose harm after the fact.

“Wisconsin should not wait for children to prove harm after systems have already seen the warning signs.”

This is not a failure to report. It is a failure to receive, retain, and act.

⚖Strengthening Civil Protections

Project Worth supports strengthening survivor centered civil protections, including higher recoverable damages in child abuse cases.

Project Worth believes child abuse cases should carry some of the highest recoverable civil damages because the harm can affect safety, mental health, education, relationships, employment, and long term wellbeing.

Unrelated financial or property based harms such as fraud, theft, property damage, business disputes, or corporate misconduct can be easier to value in dollars and may have clearer recovery pathways than grooming based child abuse claims.

⏳Statute of Limitations Reform

Project Worth supports statute of limitations reform so survivors of childhood abuse are not blocked from justice because disclosure, understanding, or safety came later.

Some states have removed civil filing deadlines for childhood sexual abuse claims or created lookback windows for survivors with previously expired claims.

Illinois allows civil actions for damages based on childhood sexual abuse to be commenced at any time. Minnesota allows civil actions for sexual abuse of an individual under 18 to be commenced at any time and previously created a Child Victims Act lookback window for older claims.

★Survivor-Centered Protocols

Survivor-centered protocols help ensure reports are documented, retained, and acted upon in ways that prioritize safety, dignity, and accountability.

When systems respond consistently, survivors are not left carrying what institutions should have addressed.
